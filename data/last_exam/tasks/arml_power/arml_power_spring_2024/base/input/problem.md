No contest material on this page. Much more is known about the function f(m, s). For examples, it is simple to see that f(km, ks) ≥ f(m, s) because any strategy for splitting up m muﬃns among s students could just be repeated k times to split up km muﬃns for ks students, and the smallest piece required wouldn’t get any smaller! It is reasonable to think, though, that the extra ﬂexibility with more muﬃns and more students might allow the smallest piece to increase in size. But this is not the case! It is not simple to prove, but f(km, ks) = f(m, s). Together with the fact proven in the contest that f(m, s) = m s f(s, m) this shows that f(m, s) can be computed for all values ofm and s just by considering those that are relatively prime and for which m > s . In fact, the problem can be turned into a 1-variable problem by considering the function g(x) = f(m, s) for x = m/s. This function is very badly behaved, and doesn’t exhibit much regularity in its graph. Check out the graphs here for some plots of g(x) over several diﬀerent intervals. The muﬃn problem is actually a fairly new mathematical puzzle, created in 2010 by a gentleman named Alan Frank. It was shared in some local puzzle forums for several years before showing up in a pamphlet of fun problems compiled by Nancy Blachman for the Julia Robinson Mathematics Festival. Some of these pamphlets found their way to the 2016 Gathering 4 Gardner conference. (Martin Gardner was one of the foremost puzzlesmiths and mathematical communicators of the 20th century. He wrote the mathematical games column for Scientiﬁc American for many years before it was taken over by G¨ odel, Escher, Bach author Douglas Hofstadter.) William Gasarch picked up a copy of the pamphlet and found the problem fun, and had his graduate students start to write programs to solve various cases. In 2018, Scott Huddleston wrote a computer program that solves the muﬃn problem very quickly (linear time complexity!) but no one had a proof that it worked correctly in all cases. That would have to wait for 2020 when Richard Chatwin solved an isomor- phic problem and proved the solution worked. His paper is available on the arXiv at arxiv.org/pdf/1907.08726.pdf. (It is not light reading!). Gasarch and his students pub- lished a book about the topic, so if you want to know everything there is to know about the muﬃn problem, here is a link to the book on Amazon.
